The Literary Quarter: Huertas 54

The house where my father was born

If my first post was about Peñuelas and how my grandparents survived the war thanks to an act of generosity that would change their lives, this second story picks up exactly where that one ended: at 54 Calle de las Huertas, in the Literary Quarter of Madrid.

That was where the Livestock Syndicate stood — the place where my grandmother María Luisa got her job as concierge, thanks to the man she had sheltered during the war. It was no ordinary job in the Spain of the post-war years: having a concierge's lodge meant having a roof, a trade and a degree of stability. For a family coming through what they had been through, it was a great deal.

Calle de las Huertas has more history than it lets on. The building of the Concejo de la Mesta — the historic headquarters of the powerful guild of transhumant shepherds that controlled Spain's drove roads for centuries — presides over the corner with Calle de León. The Livestock Syndicate that took in my family after the war was a direct heir to that tradition: the same trade, the same street, a different era.

Huertas 54: one building, many lives

The building had a life of its own inside. On the ground floor was the syndicate, and adjoining it, a small room where my grandparents lived, Nicolás and María Luisa. Above, in the building itself, there were three attic rooms. That is where their four children grew up:

  • Antonio, the eldest.
  • Mari, who was born on the very day the Civil War began.
  • Conchi.
  • My father, the youngest.

Four children in the attic rooms of a building in the Literary Quarter, in post-war Madrid. It takes no great imagination to understand that life was cramped — but full.

The cavalry comes down the stairs

Of everything from that era, there is one image my aunt Conchi always recalled with a smile. The attic rooms were up top, and the children came down the building's wooden staircase. My father, as a small boy, came down the way he came down: stumbling, jumping, no stealth possible. And my grandmother, from below, the moment she heard the first creak on the steps, would raise her voice:

— Here comes the cavalry!

That phrase was etched in my aunt Conchi's memory forever. To me it says a great deal about what my father was like as a boy, and also about my grandmother: someone who could find the funny side of small things in the middle of times that were far from easy.

Plaza de Santa Ana: my father's playground

A few steps from Huertas 54 stands Plaza de Santa Ana, one of the most open and green spaces in the neighbourhood. For the children living in the attic rooms, that square was almost an extension of home: wide, tree-lined and with room to run around. My father played there as a small child, like so many other local children who had no garden other than the street.

Plaza del Ángel and the church of San Sebastián

Nearby, Plaza del Ángel holds another family story — this time about my parents. Next to the church of San Sebastián, which has stood on that corner of the neighbourhood for centuries, is where my parents used to meet when they were young, after getting to know each other, to share a bag of quicos (roasted corn). One of those simple habits of the era that, with time, turns out to be the beginning of everything.

A neighbourhood with history, a family with more

The Literary Quarter is today one of the best-known parts of Madrid, famous for its cobbled streets and the legacy of Lope de Vega, Quevedo and Cervantes. But for me it has another layer: it is the neighbourhood where my father was a boy, where my grandmother shouted from the entrance hall, where my aunts and uncles grew up in three shared attic rooms. A story with no plaque on the wall, but as real as any other.
