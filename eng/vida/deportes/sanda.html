Shaolin Sanda

Ten years training Chinese martial arts in Madrid

From a young age I was passionate about Asian martial arts. Bruce Lee, Van Damme, Hong Kong films… the image of the disciplined, technical fighter always fascinated me. When the chance came to actually practice it, I didn't hesitate.

What is Sanda?

Sanda (also known as Sanshou, 散打) is the free-fighting component of Chinese Kung Fu and the combat modality of modern Wushu. It combines punches, kicks, takedowns and throws, and is practised with protective gear on an elevated ring called a lei tai (擂台). Unlike boxing or kickboxing, the aim is not only to strike: throwing your opponent down or out of the lei tai also scores points.

The first temple: Shaolin Temple Spain on Calle Aguilenas

My introduction to Sanda came through my cousin, who suggested we sign up together with a Groupon voucher. The school was Shaolin Temple Spain, then based on Calle Aguilenas in Madrid. So off we went.

The class hooked me from day one. The atmosphere was serious but welcoming, and the mix of technique and real contact was exactly what I was looking for. My cousin eventually moved to Motril, but I kept going.

The temple on Calle Geranios

In time, Shaolin Temple Spain moved to a new premises a couple of streets away, on Calle Geranios. The new space was larger and had a different feel: red walls, the kanji for 禅 (zen) and 武 (wu/martial) on either side, and a Buddhist altar presiding at the far end of the hall. More temple, more authentic.

Under Sifu Carlos I developed my technical game over nearly a decade: footwork, punch and kick combinations, takedowns, guard. But there was always one thing I still wanted: to compete for real, to step onto a ring. I had wanted that since childhood.

The Spanish Wushu Championship in San Martín de Valdeiglesias

In 2018 the opportunity came. The Spanish Wushu Championship was being held in San Martín de Valdeiglesias and I entered the Sanda category under 80 kg. I spent six months preparing exclusively for it — no other activity, focused entirely on physical conditioning and combat technique.

In the first round I was drawn against the Madrid school Daoiz y Velarde. The strongest school at the championship was from Galicia — several of their fighters won their categories and qualified for the European Championship the following year, held in Russia.

What stepping onto the lei tai taught me

The experience was exactly what I had wanted to live since childhood, and I have no regrets about going after it. But it was also a very concrete lesson in humility.

I had prepared well physically. Six months of specific work showed. The problem is that in this kind of event competitive experience is everything, and that is something you cannot get in the gym. Knowing how to relax your body in attacks and defences — not tensing up, not burning extra energy on every exchange — is a skill that only comes from time spent competing. Without it, your physical output skyrockets.

The result was that at 45 seconds into the first round I had nothing left. A Sanda bout is three rounds of three minutes. The difference between lasting them and not is not just fitness: it is knowing how to manage the effort, and that is pure accumulated experience.

I am very glad I did it. It was something I had wanted since I was a child, I prepared for it seriously, and I stepped onto the ring. The rest is part of the learning.

What Sanda taught me over the years

  • Discipline: Regular and demanding training over a decade
  • Respect: Towards teachers, peers and opponents
  • Control: Physical and mental control under pressure
  • Perseverance: Constantly pushing personal limits
  • Humility: Experience cannot be bought or trained — it is accumulated

The combination of football and Sanda over so many years gave me a solid foundation in physical fitness, mental discipline and competitive spirit that I bring to every aspect of my life.
